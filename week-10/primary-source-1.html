The Great Triumphal Stele of Piye
Primary Source 1
The Great Triumphal Stele of Piye
About this source: James Henry Breasted's English translation of the granite stele Piye had erected at Napata (Jebel Barkal) around 727 BC, recording his campaign against the northern Egyptian prince Tefnakht and other Delta rulers. Source: James Henry Breasted, Ancient Records of Egypt, Part IV (University of Chicago Press, 1906), Sections 816ff. Public domain. The stele is unusually long and detailed for a royal victory inscription, narrating the campaign almost as a continuous story rather than a simple list of conquests. A few words in brackets are Breasted's own bracketed restorations of damaged stone, kept here as he printed them.

On Piye's instructions to his army before entering Thebes:
"When ye arrive at Thebes, before Karnak, ye shall enter into the water, ye shall bathe in the river, ye shall dress in [fine linen], unstring the bow, loosen the arrow. Force battle upon them from afar. If he says to the infantry and chariotry of another city, 'Hasten;' (then) ye shall abide until his army comes, that ye may fight as he says."

On the fate of a defeated prince's horses:
"I swear, as Re loves me, and as my nostrils are rejuvenated with life, it is more grievous in my heart that my horses have suffered hunger, than any evil deed that thou hast done, in the prosecution of thy desire."

On princes who submitted from a distance rather than approaching in person:
"They entered not into the king's house, because they were unclean and eaters of fish; which is an abomination for the palace."

On the campaign's outcome, as Piye sailed home in triumph:
"His majesty sailed up-stream, with glad heart, the shores on his either side were jubilating... Thou makest bulls into women. Happy the heart of the mother who bore thee."

📝 EDITORIAL NOTE
The stele closes with Piye departing for Napata, in the Kushite south, rather than remaining in Egypt to govern its northern territories directly, leaving local princes in place under his overarching authority, a detail this week's Lesson 1 discusses directly. Breasted's 1906 translation remains the standard English rendering used in classrooms. A more recent scholarly translation exists in R.K. Ritner's The Libyan Anarchy (2009), but that volume is still in copyright and was not used here.
