Primary Source 1
Ashurnasirpal II's Annals
About this source: D.D. Luckenbill's English translation of the royal annals of Ashurnasirpal II (source: Ancient Records of Assyria and Babylonia, Vol. I, University of Chicago Press, 1926, public domain), paired with James B. Pritchard's translation of the separate Banquet Stele from Ashurnasirpal's new capital at Kalhu. A content note for teachers: this text describes calculated violence against rebellious cities as deliberate state policy, carved to be read as a warning. It is quoted directly here because the point of the lesson is to understand terror as a political strategy in the king's own words.

On the treatment of a city that had rebelled:
"I built a pillar over against his city gate, and I flayed all the chief men who had revolted, and I covered the pillar with their skins." Elsewhere in the same annals: "Some I impaled upon the pillar on stakes and others I bound to stakes round the pillar... Twenty men I captured alive and I immured them in the wall of his palace."

On the results the king claims for this policy:
Following each such campaign, the annals report that neighboring rulers who had not yet been attacked sent tribute of silver, gold, and fine cloth on their own before Ashurnasirpal marched against them, a pattern the annals repeat often enough that historians read it as the king's deliberate point: submission without a fight was cheaper than resistance.

On the king's building projects, presented as a mark of his glory:
Ashurnasirpal's inscriptions describe, in his own voice, building a great palace at Kalhu (modern Nimrud) and bringing cedar and cypress from the mountains along with skilled workers from conquered lands to build and decorate it.

On the feast celebrating the new capital, per Pritchard's translation of the Banquet Stele:
"When I inaugurated the palace at Calah I treated for ten days with food and drink 47,074 persons." The full inscription puts total attendance, including the people of Kalhu itself and foreign envoys, at 69,574 guests.

📝 EDITORIAL NOTE
Historians read a text like this with two things in mind at once: it is very likely an accurate record of real events, since Assyrian kings had every reason to boast about their conquests rather than invent violence that had not happened, and it is also, quite deliberately, a piece of propaganda, designed to frighten anyone who might consider rebellion before it began.
