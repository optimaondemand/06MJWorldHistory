Week 10 closed with Assyria strong enough to sack Thebes, deep inside Egyptian land, in 663 BC. This lesson goes back roughly two centuries earlier. It goes back to the king who did more than perhaps any other single ruler to build Assyria into a fearsome power. That power is what made a feat like the sack of Thebes possible. The king's name was Ashurnasirpal II. He ruled from roughly 883 to 859 BC.

📚 A VACUUM WAITING TO BE FILLED

After the Bronze Age Collapse this course studied in Week 9, Mesopotamia went more than two centuries without any one power strong enough to rule it all. Small, rival kingdoms fought for the upper hand across the region's north. None of them stayed strong enough to hold real control for long. Assyria itself had existed for a very long time, since well before Hammurabi's Babylon in Week 4. But for most of that time, it was just one regional power among several others. It often paid tribute to whichever neighbor was strongest that year.

Ashurnasirpal IIah-shur-NAH-sir-palking of Assyria from about 883 to 859 BC, who built up Assyria's army and used public terror as a tool of state. changed that. He rebuilt Assyria's army from the ground up. He moved his capital from the old religious city of Assur to a rebuilt city called Nimrudnim-ROODan ancient city on the Tigris that Ashurnasirpal II rebuilt as his royal capital., some fifty miles up the Tigris River. There, he raised a grand new palace. Huge stone statues guarded its gates: winged, human-headed bulls called lamassu. Each one was meant to frighten away evil and impress every visitor who passed beneath it. Ashurnasirpal began a string of conquests. His successors later grew these conquests into what historians call the Neo-Assyrian Empire. In time, this became the largest empire the Near East had yet seen. In other words, one determined king turned a middle-sized kingdom into the strongest power anyone in the region had ever seen.

📚 TERROR BY DESIGN

This week's Primary Source is drawn from Ashurnasirpal II's own royal writings. It records his campaigns in language that is, quite on purpose, meant to frighten. He describes skinning rebellious officials alive and hanging their skins on city walls. He describes setting captives on tall stakes outside a rebel city's gates. He describes building towers made from the skulls of defeated enemies, left standing at a city's edge as a lasting warning to travelers. He had all of these acts carved and openly recorded on palace walls and stone monuments. He did not hide them away out of shame.

This is worth pausing on directly, rather than looking past it. Ashurnasirpal was not simply cruel in some small, personal way. He used public, planned violence as a tool to rule his empire. It was a warning sent out to every city that might think about rebelling. Submit, pay tribute, and live. Or resist, and this is what will happen to you. His own annals name dozens of towns along the upper Tigris and Euphrates. Each one faced this same choice, one after another, as his armies pushed outward year after year. In other words, Ashurnasirpal was not hiding his cruelty at all. He wanted every city to hear about it, long before his army ever showed up at their gates.

📚 A QUESTION WORTH ASKING HONESTLY

This course has asked again and again whether a people's laws, customs, and habits help explain their success or failure at first-order political problems. Those problems include keeping internal peace, defending against outside threats, and binding a community together. Ashurnasirpal's terror clearly served the first two goals, at least for a time. Cities that had seen what happened to a rebellious neighbor thought twice before rising up themselves. Assyria's borders grew quickly and held firm for the rest of his reign.

But the third goal is harder to judge. Could terror bind a large, many-peopled empire into something like a shared political community? This lesson will not answer that fully yet. Fear can win a battle. Fear can even hold a province for a while. But can fear alone hold an empire together across generations, long after the king who inspired it is gone? Week 12 answers that question directly, when this same Assyrian empire, at the very height of its power, falls apart far faster than anyone watching it at its peak might have guessed. In other words, winning wars is not the same thing as building lasting loyalty. This lesson leaves that second, harder problem open on purpose.

⚙️ TECHNOLOGY SPOTLIGHT · ASSYRIA'S PROFESSIONAL STANDING ARMY
Photo: via Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

Assyria's year-round army, backed by iron weapons, siege engines, and cavalry, was a genuinely new kind of military institution in the ancient Near East, distinct from the seasonal levies of Sumer, Akkad, or Egypt this course has studied so far. It let Assyria's kings campaign nearly every year without waiting for harvests to end, and it is the single biggest reason Assyria's empire grew as large, and as fast, as it did.

📚 BUILDING THE MACHINE

Ashurnasirpal's terror was backed by a truly new kind of army. Earlier armies this course has studied, in Sumer, Akkad, or even New Kingdom Egypt, leaned heavily on seasonal levies. Farmers and townsmen were called up for one campaign, then sent home again once the fighting ended. Assyria built something different instead: a professional standing armya year-round, trained military force, kept ready at all times, rather than farmers and townsmen called up only for a single campaign.. Its soldiers were kept ready and paid all year round, rather than sent back to their fields. Its troops carried iron weapons rather than bronze, a real edge in strength over rivals still equipped the older way.

Special building crews built siege enginesa large machine, such as a battering ram or a tall movable tower, built to break through a city's walls.: battering rams and tall movable towers. These could break through even strong, thick city walls. Earlier armies could often only starve such a city into surrender, through a long blockade. Skilled cavalryKAV-uhl-reesoldiers who fight while mounted on horseback. units gave Assyria's kings something new too: soldiers fighting from horseback, rather than on foot or from chariots alone. This gave Assyria a fast-moving strike force that earlier Near Eastern armies had rarely fielded at this scale. Assyrian kings after Ashurnasirpal kept building and expanding this standing, professional machine for nearly two centuries. It is what let Assyria's later rulers reach as far as Egypt itself, sacking Thebes in the very campaign this week's opening lines described. In other words, Assyria did not just get lucky, over and over, in battle after battle. It built a genuinely new kind of army, one made to fight all year, every year, without stopping for the harvest.
