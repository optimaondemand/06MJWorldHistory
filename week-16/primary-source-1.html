💡 ABOUT THIS SOURCE
Samuel Butler's 1898 prose translation of Homer's Iliad, Book 1, quoted directly. Public domain; hosted in full at Wikisource, the same translation and translator already used for Week 8's Catalogue of Ships excerpt.
📖 ON THE POEM'S OWN FRAMING OF THE COST, FROM ITS OPENING LINE

"Sing, O goddess, the anger of Achilles son of Peleus, that brought countless ills upon the Achaeans."

📖 ON AGAMEMNON'S THREAT TO SEIZE BRISEIS

"I shall come to your tent and take your own prize Briseis, that you may learn how much stronger I am than you are, and that another may fear to set himself up as equal or comparable with me."

📖 ON ACHILLES'S DECISION TO WITHDRAW

"Now, therefore, I shall go back to Phthia; it will be much better for me to return home with my ships, for I will not stay here dishonoured to gather gold and substance for you."

📝 EDITORIAL NOTE
This excerpt is drawn from the very opening of the Iliad, and its first word in the original Greek is "wrath." Homer states outright, before the story even properly begins, that Achilles's anger will cost many lives. This course studies this episode not to determine whether the quarrel was historically real, which cannot be confirmed, but to examine what the poem itself says about pride, honor, and the real damage an otherwise genuinely courageous person can cause when wounded pride goes unchecked.
