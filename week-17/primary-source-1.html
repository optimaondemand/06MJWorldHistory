💡 ABOUT THIS SOURCE
A modern scholarly translation of the Cyrus Cylinder, a clay cylinder inscribed in Akkadian cuneiform and buried in Babylon's foundations following Cyrus's conquest of the city in 539 BC. No public-domain translation of this text exists (the cylinder was not fully translated until 20th-century Assyriology, and scholarship on it continues to be revised); the excerpts below follow Mordechai Cogan's translation as adapted for Livius.org (itself based on H. Schaudig's critical edition), cross-checked against the British Museum's own modern translation (Irving Finkel and colleagues) for accuracy.
📖 ON CYRUS'S CHOSEN STATUS

"[Marduk] examined and checked all the entirety of the lands… he took a righteous king, his favorite, by the hand, he called out his name: Cyrus, king of Anshan; he pronounced his name to be king all over the world."

📖 ON THE ENTRY INTO BABYLON

"He made him enter his city Babylon without fighting or battle; he saved Babylon from hardship. He delivered Nabonidus, the king who did not revere him, into his hands."

📖 ON RESTORING DISPLACED PEOPLES AND THEIR GODS

"I returned the images of the gods, who had resided there, to their places and I let them dwell in eternal abodes. I gathered all their inhabitants and returned to them their dwellings."

📝 EDITORIAL NOTE
The Cyrus Cylinder has become widely, and inaccurately, described in modern times as the world's first human rights charter, and was even displayed at the United Nations under that description. Read in its own historical context, it is a fairly conventional Mesopotamian royal building and restoration inscription, closely related to Nebuchadnezzar's inscriptions studied in Week 13, presenting Cyrus's conquest and policies as pleasing to Marduk in exactly the self-praising style nearly every Mesopotamian king used about his own reign. What is genuinely significant is not its language but its substance: outside evidence confirms that Cyrus really did pursue a deliberate policy of returning displaced peoples and restoring their gods and shrines, a real and consequential difference from Assyrian and Babylonian practice studied earlier in this course.
