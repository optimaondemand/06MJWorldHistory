Every civilization this course has studied so far has left behind a fairly steady written record of its own rise. This week's subject, the Medes, are different. That difference is itself worth understanding before their story can even be told.

📚 A PEOPLE ALMOST INVISIBLE IN THE RECORD

The Medes were an Iranian-speaking people. They settled in and around the Zagros MountainsZAG-rosa long, rugged mountain range east of Mesopotamia, home to the Medes and later the Persians., the rugged highland region east of Mesopotamia, sometime after the Bronze Age Collapse this course studied in Week 9. For roughly the next four hundred years, from about 1150 to 728 BC, the Medes appear in the historical record only faintly and now and then. Mostly, they show up as one tribute-paying people among many along Assyria's eastern border. This is the same kind of relationship this course studied directly through Jehu of Israel in Week 11. No Median king from this long stretch left behind writings of his own, the way Assyrian, Babylonian, or Egyptian kings did. This course simply does not have a Median version of Ashurnasirpal's annals or Piye's stele for these centuries. It is worth being honest about that gap, rather than pretend otherwise. In other words, this course cannot tell the Medes' story the same way it told Egypt's or Assyria's.

The Medes are not entirely silent in outside sources, though. Assyrian kings, including Sargon II and Sennacherib, record campaigns against Median chiefs in their own royal writings, from the eighth and seventh centuries BC. They call these chiefs the "Mada." These brief mentions confirm that Median communities existed. They also show that Medes sometimes resisted Assyrian power. But they tell this course almost nothing about how the Medes organized themselves. They also do not say who, if anyone, ruled the Medes as a single people during this long stretch of time. In other words, outsiders saw the Medes only from a distance, and only now and then.

This kind of near-invisibility is worth pausing on. It does not mean nothing was happening among the Medes during these four centuries. Communities were forming. Tribal and family structures were organizing local life. Slowly, something like shared political identity was taking shape. All of this happened outside the notice of nearby empires that kept written records. A people can be building toward something big for a very long time before that process becomes visible to history at all. In other words, silence in the record is not the same thing as nothing happening.

📚 DEIOCES, ACCORDING TO HERODOTUS

This week's first Primary Source turns to the Greek historian Herodotus, already introduced in Week 10. His book gives the fullest surviving account of how the Medes are said to have first united under a single ruler.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY

Herodotus tells a truly memorable story. A Median villager named Deiocesdee-OH-seeza Median villager Herodotus credits with uniting the Medes under a single king, becoming their first ruler. was known locally for his fairness in settling disputes. He became so trusted that people from beyond his own village began bringing him their conflicts to judge. According to Herodotus, Deioces then deliberately stopped judging cases altogether. Disorder among the Medes grew worse without him. Eventually, the Medes themselves gathered and chose him as their first king. Once in power, Deioces is said to have built a grand, carefully guarded court at a new capital, Ecbatanaek-BAT-uh-nuhthe capital city Herodotus says Deioces built for the united Medes, in the Zagros highlands.. Herodotus describes this capital as ringed by seven walls, one inside another. Each wall was painted its own color: white, black, deep red, blue, and bright orange. The two innermost walls were plated in silver and gold. He surrounded himself with strict rules, even forbidding laughter in his presence. This was meant to keep the nobles from feeling too familiar with him. Not long before, after all, they had been his own equals, and he did not want them plotting against him. Herodotus also names six Median tribes that are said to have come together under Deioces's rule. One of them was called the Magi. This priestly tribe would later play a major religious role under the Persian kings this course meets in Weeks 17 and 18. Keep that name in mind. It will come up again.

This is a wonderful story, and it needs to be read the way Week 10 taught this course to read Herodotus generally. It may preserve a real memory of how Median kingship actually began. If so, it began from below, built through a real reputation for fairness rather than conquest. That would make it a very different kind of origin story than most kingdoms this course has studied. It may also be, in whole or in part, a legendary account, shaped over generations of retelling before Herodotus ever wrote it down. This is the same caution this course applied to Sargon's basket in Week 3, or Gilgamesh's flood in Week 2. Modern historians still remain divided on how much of Deioces's story reflects real history. They also debate how much of it grew in the retelling. Two full centuries passed between his supposed reign and the day Herodotus wrote it all down. The honest answer, once again, is that this course simply cannot be sure. Pretending otherwise would be worse than admitting the uncertainty directly. In other words, an honest "we don't know" is better than a confident guess.

📚 CLOSING

Whatever its true origins, a united Median kingdom did come to exist. Its rise from a barely visible frontier people to a major regional power is one of this course's clearest examples yet of a pattern worth naming directly. That pattern is simple: slowly, then all at once. Lesson 2 picks up exactly there.
