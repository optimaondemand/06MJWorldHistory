Herodotus on Deioces and the Founding of Media
Primary Source 1
Herodotus, Histories Book I (on Deioces and the Founding of Media)
About this source: George Rawlinson's 1858-1860 English translation of Herodotus's Histories, Book I, sections 96-101, describing the legendary founding of a unified Median kingdom under a ruler named Deioces. Public domain; confirmed via a hosted transcription (York University, credited to Rawlinson).

On Deioces's early reputation for justice:
"As the Medes at that time dwelt in scattered villages without any central authority, and lawlessness in consequence prevailed throughout the land, Deioces... applied himself with greater zeal and earnestness than ever before to the practice of justice among his fellows."

On his withdrawal and its consequences:
"Deioces, feeling himself now all important, announced that he did not intend any longer to hear causes, and appeared no more in the seat in which he had been accustomed to sit and administer justice."

On his selection as king:
"When this debate began the claims of Deioces and his praises were at once in every mouth; so that presently all agreed that he should be king."

On the ceremonial isolation he established once in power:
"He allowed no one to have direct access to the person of the king, but made all communication pass through the hands of messengers, and forbade the king to be seen by his subjects. He also made it an offence for any one whatsoever to laugh or spit in the royal presence."

📝 EDITORIAL NOTE
Herodotus wrote this account more than a century after the events it describes, drawing on stories passed down rather than any surviving Median royal inscription. Modern historians remain genuinely divided over how much of Deioces's story reflects real history and how much is legend that grew in the retelling, a caution this week's Lesson 1 discusses directly.
