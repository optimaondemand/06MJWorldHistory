📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What do two rival cities look like before either one knows the other exists?

Somewhere in the eighth century BC, by the old count ancient people themselves used, two cities were founded on opposite shores of the central Mediterranean. Neither city was much to look at yet. Neither founder had any idea the other city even existed. Neither knew what it would someday become. Centuries later, their own people would fight three long wars over which of them would survive at all. This week's two Primary Sources tell each city's own legendary story of how it began.

📚 DIDO AND THE NEW CITY

This week's first Primary Source concerns Carthage, the greatest of the Phoenician colonies Lesson 1 introduced. It was planted on the North African coast, not far from modern Tunis. Its own name, in Phoenician, meant simply "New City." The oldest surviving version of its founding story comes from a later writer named Justin. In it, a Tyrian princess named DidoDY-doha Tyrian princess said to have fled Phoenicia and founded Carthage around 814 BC., sometimes called Elissa, fled her home city. Her own brother, King Pygmalion of Tyre, had killed her husband for his wealth. Dido sailed west with a band of loyal followers. She struck a shrewd, tricky deal with the local Berber ruler over how much land her new settlement could claim. Through that deal, she founded Carthage on the North African shore. Old tradition dates this founding to 814 BC.

Centuries later, the Roman poet Virgil told a very different version of Dido's story. His epic, the Aeneid, was written under the emperor Augustus. In Virgil's telling, Dido falls in love with the Trojan hero Aeneas, blown ashore at Carthage while fleeing the fall of Troy. She kills herself in despair when he sails on toward Italy to found the line that will one day become Rome. This version is fine writing, but it cannot be real history, and this course should say so plainly. The Trojan War, whatever its real kernel, belongs to an age centuries before 814 BC. That is the very Bronze Age this course studied collapsing back in Week 9. Virgil knew this well. He was not making a mistake. He was writing centuries after Rome and Carthage had already fought their real wars. He used this anachronism, a detail placed in the wrong time on purpose, to suggest that Rome's later hate for Carthage was somehow set into both cities' fates from the very start. It is a poet's point, not a historian's claim, and that gap matters.

📚 ROMULUS, REMUS, AND A CITY BUILT ON A KILLING

This week's second Primary Source turns to Rome's own founding legend, kept most fully by the Roman historian Livy. According to Livy, twin brothers named RomulusROM-yoo-lustwin brother, with Remus, whose legend credits them with founding Rome in 753 BC. and RemusREE-mustwin brother, with Romulus, whose legend credits them with founding Rome in 753 BC. were born to Rhea Silvia. Her uncle Amulius had seized the throne of the nearby city of Alba Longa from her father, Numitor. Amulius forced Rhea Silvia into a role meant to leave her childless, so she could never bear a rival to his throne. Fearing the twins anyway as future rivals, Amulius ordered them drowned in the Tiber RiverTY-burthe river in central Italy on whose banks Rome was traditionally founded.. The basket carrying them instead washed ashore near the Palatine HillPAL-uh-tyneone of Rome's seven hills, where tradition says the she-wolf found and nursed Romulus and Remus.. There, tradition says, a she-wolf nursed the boys. A shepherd named Faustulus later found the twins and raised them as his own sons.

Notice how closely this echoes a story this course already knows well. An infant is set adrift on a river. He is saved against every chance. He is meant for far more than his own humble rescuers could have guessed. That is almost exactly the shape of Sargon's basket story from Week 3. This does not prove that one story copied the other. It shows instead that this kind of story, a low, uncertain start hiding a great future, appealed to ancient people again and again, in far-apart cultures with no contact at all.

Grown to manhood, Romulus and Remus restored their grandfather Numitor to his throne. They then set out to found a city of their own, near where they had washed ashore. Rome itself rose in a part of Italy already shared with other peoples. Etruscan cities lay just to the north, and their customs shaped early Rome deeply. Greek trading colonies lay further south, in Sicily and along Italy's coast. The Greeks themselves called this whole southern cluster Magna GraeciaMAG-nuh GREE-shuh"Greater Greece," the Greek trading colonies of southern Italy and Sicily., "Greater Greece." Livy's account of the twins does not end happily, though. The brothers argued bitterly over exactly where their new city's sacred boundary line, the pomerium, should run. Remus mocked his brother by leaping over the low wall Romulus had begun to mark out. In Livy's telling, Romulus killed his own twin brother on the spot for it. He said the same fate awaited anyone else who dared cross Rome's border uninvited. Tradition dates this founding, and this fratricide, to 753 BC. Rome, in other words, begins its own story with a killing its own writers never tried to hide.

🏙️ CITIES · CARTHAGE AND ROME

Carthage, on the North African coast, and Rome, on the Tiber River in central Italy, were both founded as small, precarious settlements in the eighth century BC. Neither city's founders could have guessed the two would one day fight for control of the whole Mediterranean.

📚 CLOSING

Set side by side, these two founding stories share a striking pattern: exile, hardship, and a small, uncertain settlement built by people fleeing worse trouble elsewhere. Neither Dido's followers nor Romulus's shepherds could have imagined that their two cities would someday stand as the Mediterranean's two greatest rival powers. That struggle would not begin in earnest for another five and a half centuries, and it lies well beyond where this course leaves off. In other words, this course leaves both cities here, at their small and shaky start, long before either one becomes a name the whole Mediterranean world would fear or admire. What belongs here is the beginning: two foundling cities, born the same rough century, each certain only of its own small patch of shore.
