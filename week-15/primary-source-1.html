The Legendary Founding of Carthage
Primary Source 1
The Legendary Founding of Carthage (Dido, per Virgil and Justin)
Primary Source Reading
💡 ABOUT THIS SOURCE
Two genuine translations, quoted directly: John Selby Watson's 1853 English translation of Justin's Epitome of the Philippic History of Pompeius Trogus, Book 18, and John Dryden's 1697 translation of Virgil's Aeneid, Book 4. Both are in the public domain.
📖 JUSTIN'S ACCOUNT (WATSON'S TRANSLATION), ON DIDO'S FLIGHT FROM TYRE

Justin names Dido's murdered husband Acerbas, a wealthy priest killed by her brother King Pygmalion for his hidden treasure. Watson's translation records that Dido (called Elissa in this account) "secretly contrived a mode of flight, admitting into her confidence some of the leading men of the city" before escaping by sea.

📖 JUSTIN'S ACCOUNT, ON FOUNDING THE NEW CITY

"[She] directed the hide to be cut into the thinnest possible strips, and thus acquired a greater portion of ground than she had apparently demanded; whence the place had afterwards the name of Byrsa" (Byrsa, meaning "hide," became the name of Carthage's citadel hill).

📖 VIRGIL'S ACCOUNT (DRYDEN'S TRANSLATION), ON DIDO'S CURSE BEFORE HER DEATH

"Perpetual Hate, and mortal Wars proclaim, / Against the Prince, the People, and the Name," Dido cries, calling for "some Avenger of our Lybian Blood, / With Fire and Sword" to pursue Aeneas's descendants. She then "mounts the Fun'ral Pile, with furious haste," and, embracing Aeneas's sword, "struck: Deep enter'd in her side / The piercing Steel, with reeking Purple dy'd."

📝 EDITORIAL NOTE
These two traditions come from very different times and purposes. Justin's version, tracing back to the earlier Greek historian Timaeus, preserves what is likely the older, more historically grounded core of Carthage's founding story, dated by ancient tradition to 814 BC. Virgil wrote his version roughly eight hundred years later, under the Roman emperor Augustus, and deliberately placed Dido in the same era as the Trojan War, a period centuries before Carthage actually existed. This is not a historical error on Virgil's part. It is a poet's device, using an impossible meeting between Dido and Aeneas to suggest that Rome and Carthage's later rivalry, which Roman audiences already knew the outcome of, was somehow fated from the very beginning. Sourcing note: the opening detail of Justin's account (Pygmalion's murder of Acerbas) is confirmed accurate in content from secondary scholarly citation but was not retrieved word-for-word from Watson's own translation; a follow-up pass should confirm the exact Watson wording directly against a hosted copy of Book 18.
