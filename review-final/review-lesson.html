📖 LOOKING BACK ACROSS EIGHTEEN WEEKS

This course began with a simple question. It is so basic that it is easy to forget someone once had to answer it for the very first time: why farm at all? Eighteen weeks have passed since then. That one question led us through the world's first cities, its first empires, a real collapse, and a new empire big enough to hold three river valleys at once. Before this course ends for the semester, it is worth stepping back to look at the whole shape of this story together.

📚 PART 1 · FARMING, CITIES, AND THE FIRST KINGS

The story opened at Jericho and Çatalhöyük. There, families first chose to stay in one place and grow crops, instead of following herds and wild grain across the seasons. This course was honest about one thing: that choice did not obviously make life easier at first. Still, it led somewhere new. Farming produced extra food, more than a family needed just to survive. That surplus fed Sumer's walled cities. The greatest of these cities was Uruk, the first true city this course studied. Cities needed record-keepers, and record-keepers needed a way to write things down. That way was cuneiform, the wedge-shaped script we have now read across four thousand years of Mesopotamian history. We saw it first in the Sumerian King List. We saw it again in Ashurbanipal's library. We saw it one final time in the Behistun Inscription itself.

Out of Sumer's rival cities rose the world's first empire, built by Sargon of Akkad. Legend says his life began when he was set adrift on a river, alone in a basket of reeds. Here is a question worth pausing on: what if that basket had sunk? There is no clear reason to think another Sargon would have appeared just when he did, or ruled the way he did. All semester, this course has argued the same point. Empires are not owed to history. They depend on real people making real choices, at moments that could easily have gone another way.

A century or two later, Hammurabi of Babylon faced the same problem Sargon once solved: how do you hold a kingdom together? He gave a different answer. Instead of relying mainly on conquest, he offered his kingdom a public code of law. This is one of this course's clearest tests of an idea repeated all semester: that stable offices and steady, predictable justice are what let a large community hold together over time.

📚 PART 2 · EGYPT, AND THE WORLD BEYOND THE TWO RIVERS

While Mesopotamia's kings rose and fell, Egypt built something unusually lasting under one crown. Narmer joined Egypt together first. Later kings expressed that unity in the Old Kingdom's pyramids. Still later, rulers like Hatshepsut and Thutmose III carried Egypt's power forward, but by two very different roads. Hatshepsut chose trade and diplomacy. Thutmose III chose conquest. Neither path proved simply better than the other, a pattern this course would see again and again. This course also stepped briefly outside its main Near Eastern and Egyptian story, to meet the planned streets of Mohenjo-daro and the oracle bones of Shang China. That side trip was a reminder: this semester's main story was never the only ancient story worth telling. It was only the one this course chose to follow most closely.

📚 PART 3 · A BRONZE WORLD, AND ITS COLLAPSE

By the time this course reached Crete's palaces, Mycenaean Greece, and the Hittite Empire, a whole connected Bronze Age world had formed. Trade, marriage alliances, and treaties bound it together, including the treaty Egypt and Hatti signed after the Battle of Kadesh. Then, in little more than a single lifetime, that world came apart.

This course was careful not to blame that collapse on one single cause. Instead, it weighed drought, internal unrest, and broken trade networks together, rather than pointing to one dramatic villain. Here is a second question worth asking, the same one this course asked back in Week 9: what if the raiders historians call the Sea Peoples had struck two hundred years earlier, or two hundred years later, than they actually did? A world already weakened by other pressures met them at its weakest possible moment. A stronger world, or one caught at a different moment, might have absorbed the shock the way Egypt eventually did, wounded but still standing.

📚 PART 4 · FILLING THE VACUUM

Out of that collapse's wreckage rose the powers of Quarter 2. Kushite kings ruled Egypt from the south. Assyria built an army of iron-armed, full-time soldiers. Ashurbanipal filled a great library at Nineveh, a city that later fell in 612 BC. Nebuchadnezzar built Babylon into a wonder, then destroyed Jerusalem. The Medes rose slowly, over centuries, before suddenly becoming a great power. Each of these four powers solved the same basic problems in its own way. Assyria relied on terror. Babylon relied on splendor and law. Media relied on patient, slow endurance. Each approach carried real strength, and each also carried a real cost.

📖 PART 5 · ONE EMPIRE, AT LAST

That is the arc this course closes on. Cyrus the Great is another figure legend places in danger as a baby. Ask yourself a third question: what if the infant Cyrus had not survived being left exposed, the way legend says his own grandfather Astyages ordered? Cyrus did survive. He grew up to unite Media and Persia, then conquer Lydia, then take Babylon itself, largely by offering conquered peoples less punishment than Assyria or Babylon ever had.

For the first time, Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Persia's own homeland all answered to one ruler. That empire does not stop there, either. It already rules Greek cities along the Anatolian coast. Just across the Aegean Sea sit the small, quarreling city-states of mainland Greece. What happens next, between that empire and those Greek cities, is a story for another semester.
