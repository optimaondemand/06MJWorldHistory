💡 ABOUT THIS SOURCE
George Rawlinson's 1858-1860 translation of Herodotus's Histories, Book III, quoted directly, describing Cambyses's conduct after conquering Egypt. Public domain; confirmed via cross-referenced quotations of Rawlinson's translation (Wikisource hosts the full text at The History of Herodotus (Rawlinson)/Book 3), cross-checked against G.C. Macaulay's translation of the same passage for consistency.
📖 ON THE TREATMENT OF THE APIS BULL, CAMBYSES'S MOST NOTORIOUS ACT AFTER CONQUERING EGYPT

"Cambyses, like the harebrained person that he was, drew his dagger, and aimed at the belly of the animal, but missed his mark, and stabbed him in the thigh. Then he laughed, and said thus to the priests: 'Oh! blockheads, and think ye that gods become like this, of flesh and blood, and sensible to steel? A fit god indeed for Egyptians, such an one! But it shall cost you dear that you have made me your laughing-stock.'"

📖 ON THE BULL'S DEATH AND ITS CONCEALMENT

"Apis, wounded in the thigh, lay some time pining in the temple; at last he died of his wound, and the priests buried him secretly without the knowledge of Cambyses."

📝 EDITORIAL NOTE
Herodotus wrote this account roughly a century after Cambyses's reign, and much of his information likely came from Egyptian sources with strong reasons to resent the Persian conqueror who had just ended their native dynasty. No Persian account of Cambyses's own reign, comparable to the Cyrus Cylinder or the Behistun Inscription, survives to weigh against this hostile portrait. This does not mean every detail is false, but it does mean this account should be read as one side of a story this course cannot fully verify, not as a neutral record of Cambyses's character.
