📚 THE GREAT PYRAMID

Around 2560 BC, under the pharaoh KhufuKOO-foothe pharaoh under whom the Great Pyramid of Giza was built, ruling roughly 2589 to 2566 BC., workers finished the Great Pyramid of Giza. It first rose about 481 feet high. It was built from more than two million blocks of limestone and granite. Some granite blocks, hauled from quarries at Aswan far to the south, weighed as much as 80 tons each. The Greek historian Herodotus later claimed the pyramid took twenty years to build, though modern experts still debate the exact number.

For a long time, popular stories pictured this as the work of huge crowds of enslaved workers driven by the whip. Evidence dug up from workers' villages near the pyramids tells a new story. Diggers found bakeries, breweries, and animal bones nearby. These finds show that pyramid workers ate a solid diet that included beer and meat. They lived in planned camps, not scattered huts. The workforce seems to have included skilled full-time builders. Alongside them worked changing crews of Egyptian farmers. These farmers were likely called up for a set season each year. This was likely during the months of the inundationin-un-DAY-shunthe yearly flood of the Nile River, which covered nearby farmland with rich, dark soil., when Nile floodwaters left the fields underwater anyway. Some work crews even carved their own team names into the stone blocks they moved. Names like "Friends of Khufu" turn up on blocks today, a small, human trace of pride left behind by common laborers.

None of this means the work was easy or freely chosen the way a modern job is. But it was organized, paid, state labor. It was not simple mass slavery, and that popular mistake is worth correcting plainly rather than letting it stand. In 1954, workers clearing rubble near the pyramid found a complete wooden boat, taken apart and sealed inside a pit. It had likely been built for Khufu's own journey through the afterlife. Once put back together, the boat stretched more than 140 feet long, its ancient cedar planks still sound enough to fit together again.

Building the pyramid took great skill and precision. Its sides line up with true north within a tiny fraction of a degree. Khufu's engineers had no compass or telescope. They used only careful eyesight, surveying tools, and star sightings to reach that precision. Ramps, levers, and copper tools, not any wheeled machine, moved and shaped the stone. Whatever else can be argued about the Old Kingdom, one thing stands out. Its power to organize labor, supplies, and exact engineering skill on this scale, kept up across decades of rule, was real. Nothing else on Earth matched it at the time.

📚 PTAHHOTEP'S ADVICE

Not every Old Kingdom text is a royal monument. The Instructions of Ptahhotep, one of the oldest surviving wisdom texts anywhere in the world, is written as advice from an aging viziervih-ZEERa pharaoh's top official, in charge of running the day-to-day business of government. to his son and successor. The full text survives today mainly through a later copy called the Prisse Papyrus. Historians are not fully sure just when Ptahhoteptah-HOH-tepa vizier whose collected wise sayings, the Instructions of Ptahhotep, are among the oldest surviving wisdom writing in the world. lived. The text names him as vizier to a king called Djedkare Isesi, which would place him late in the Old Kingdom, well over a century after Khufu. Egyptian tradition later said Ptahhotep lived to the legendary age of 110 years, a symbol of the wisdom his advice claimed to offer.

Rather than boasting of conquest or divine favor, Ptahhotep's advice is about everyday virtue. It covers how to listen well. It covers how a powerful man should treat those below him without pride. It covers how to control one's temper, and how to remember that wisdom can come from anyone, no matter their rank. This week's primary source lets you read some of that advice firsthand, written down long ago in hieroglyphicshy-ruh-GLIF-iksEgypt's early writing system, made of small pictures that stood for sounds, words, or ideas. on papyrus. Notice how different this text feels from a pharaoh's grand monument. Where Narmer's Palette and a pyramid's huge size show power outward, Ptahhotep's instructions look inward. They point at the private, personal virtues, courtesy, self-control, and patience, that Egyptian culture expected even from its most powerful men.

📚 DIVINE KINGSHIP, HELD IN TENSION

Taken together, this week's two lessons present a pharaoh who was two things at once. On one hand, he was treated as a living god in charge of keeping ma'at across the entire universe. On the other hand, his own officials wrote guidance on ordinary human virtues like patience and self-control. These do not have to be opposites. A king understood to stand for cosmic order might be expected, just because of that role, to model the same personal order and self-government at every level. That standard would run from the pharaoh's own household down to the humblest farmer's.

Compare this again to Mesopotamia. Hammurabi backed his rule with a promise of justice enforced through law. Egypt's Old Kingdom pharaoh backed his rule with a claimed link to the divine itself. This lesson suggests that claim was backed up by a call for personal virtue, not law alone. Keep watching for how this claim to divine kingship holds up, or comes under strain, as Egypt's story continues in the weeks ahead.

⚙️ TECHNOLOGY SPOTLIGHT · DIVINE KINGSHIP
Photo: Jon Bodsworth, copyrighted free use, via Wikimedia Commons.

Egyptian kingship claimed something Mesopotamian kingship never quite claimed for itself. A Sumerian or Akkadian king ruled with the gods' favor, chosen and backed by them, but still a man set apart from the gods themselves. Egypt's pharaoh, from the very start of the unified kingdom, was treated as an actual living god on earth, an image of the falcon god Horus, responsible for keeping ma'at, the truth and balance holding the whole universe together. This is a truly different idea of kingship, one this course will keep comparing against Mesopotamia's model as both civilizations' stories continue.
