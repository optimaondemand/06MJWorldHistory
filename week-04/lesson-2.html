Hammurabi had a stele, a tall stone monument, carved with 282 laws and set up in a temple where his subjects could see it, or have it read to them. This week's primary sources let you read a selection of those laws directly, along with the code's prologue, in which Hammurabi describes his own purpose. This lesson gives you the tools to evaluate what you will read there.

📚 JUDGING A REGIME BY WHAT IT SOLVES

One good way historians judge an ancient government is to ask a very real question. Does it solve the basic problems every group of people faces? Does it set up stable offices whose power people actually trust? Does it apply common laws in a steady, fair way, rather than leaving justice to the whims of whoever happens to hold power?

Judged by these standards, Hammurabi's Code is a real win. It covers a huge range of daily life: contracts, wages, marriage, inheritance, and the duties of doctors, builders, and boat-builders. A trader, a farmer, or a family with a complaint had, at least in theory, a public, written rule to point to, rather than trusting only the private word of whichever local strongman happened to be in charge.

📚 "AN EYE FOR AN EYE": PROPORTIONAL JUSTICE

The Code's most famous rule is eye-for-an-eye justice (lex talionis), Latin for "law of getting even." Under this rule, the punishment should match the harm done: an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth, a broken bone for a broken bone. Feuds and revenge killings, before written law, could spin far out past the size of the first wrong. Against that backdrop, lex talionis is a real limit on revenge. The law caps what a wronged side is allowed to take back, rather than leaving revenge open-ended.

📚 A REAL, BUT LIMITED, JUSTICE

At the same time, a close read of the Code shows something important: its justice was not the same for everyone. Babylonian society was split into set classes. The free citizen (amelu) held high or full legal rank. The commoner (mushkenum) was a free but lower class, with fewer legal shields, usually less than what an amelu paid or got. Slaves held the legal status of property under the law, not full legal rights as a person.

The same wound, an eye put out, a bone broken, carried a different fine, based on the class of both the victim and the one at fault. Hammurabi's Code gave real, steady, publicly known justice. At the very same time, it built unequal treatment right into its own bones. Both things are true at once, and a fair look at the Code has to hold both.

⚙️ TECHNOLOGY SPOTLIGHT · BABYLONIAN MATHEMATICS
Photo: Columbia University, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

Babylonian math grew right out of the same money-keeping needs that gave us cuneiform writing back in Week 2. Babylonian scribes used a sexagesimal system, based on 60 rather than 10, which made it easy to split numbers into thirds, quarters, fifths, and sixths without messy fractions, part of why the number 60 still shows up in how we measure time and angles today. The clay tablet Plimpton 322, listing what math experts now call Pythagorean triples, shows scribes using a form of the Pythagorean theorem more than a thousand years before Pythagoras lived in Greece. This math grew out of the same scribe schools that trained the men who wrote laws like Hammurabi's Code.

⚙️ TECHNOLOGY SPOTLIGHT · SOCIAL CLASS UNDER THE CODE

Hammurabi's Code named three broad classes of person, setting different fines and pay depending on which class was in a given case. The amelu held full legal rank: the top shields, and often the top fines for wronging one. The mushkenum, a free but lower class, got real legal shields, but at a lower level, with less pay owed for harm against them. Enslaved people held the least legal rank of all, treated under the law more like goods than like full legal persons, though the Code still set some small shields around their care. This layered system shows something key about Babylonian justice: it was real and steady, but it was never equal.

📚 KINGSHIP JUSTIFIED BY JUSTICE

Finally, notice how Hammurabi frames his own right to rule. He does not just claim power by force of arms. In the Code's prologue, which you will read this week, Hammurabi presents himself as chosen by the gods, sent to "cause justice to prevail in the land," and to protect the weak from the strong. A king who bases his rule on justice, rather than on raw force alone, sets a real standard his own subjects, and history after them, can measure him against.
