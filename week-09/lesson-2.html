🗺️ SECONDARY MAP · WHAT SURVIVED AND WHAT DID NOT
Map: via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

This map compares Bronze Age capitals that were destroyed in the collapse, including Hattusa and Pylos, against ones that survived, wounded, including Memphis and Babylon.

Ramesses III's Medinet Habu inscription, this week's first Primary Source, tells the Bronze Age Collapse's story from the winning side. Egypt was a great power that survived a direct assault and lived to celebrate its own victory in carved stone, for its own subjects and for later generations to read. This lesson's Primary Source tells the same crisis from the losing side, through the ordinary paperwork of a palace government that had no idea it was living through its final weeks.

📚 THE VIEW FROM PYLOS

At Pylos, one of the Mycenaean palace centers Week 8 introduced, archaeologists recovered a set of clay tablets. The American archaeologist Carl Blegen began digging at Pylos in 1939. He found the very first tablet on his very first day at the site, a stroke of luck that quickly led to hundreds more. These tablets were hardened and preserved, almost as a strange gift, by the very fire that destroyed the palace around 1180 BC. Nearly a thousand tablets survive from Pylos alone, one of the largest caches of Linear B writing ever found. Among them are records historians call the rower tablets: lists organizing men, likely rowers for warships, and assigning officers to watch posts along the coastline.

These are not poetry. They are not royal boasts. They do not even clearly state what danger the palace expected. They are exactly the kind of dry, practical bookkeeping a palace economy, as Week 8 described, produced all the time as a matter of routine: names, numbers, place assignments. Read alongside what we now know happened next, they carry an unmistakable weight. This was a government mobilizing its coastal defenses. It was most likely acting in real fear of an attack from the sea, in the final months before its palace burned and Mycenaean rule in this region ended for good. One tablet, known to scholars simply as An 657, lists rowers assigned watch duty at points all along the coast, group by group, the closest thing this archive offers to a battle plan.

📚 A GOVERNMENT THAT DID NOT KNOW ITS OWN ENDING

This is worth sitting with directly. Whoever wrote these tablets was doing ordinary office work. It was the same kind of work Linear B scribes had been doing for a long time: counting men and assigning posts. They had no sense that they were recording the last acts of a civilization. Other tablets from these same final months record ordinary religious offerings, oil and wine sent out to local shrines, exactly as they had been every year before. History rarely announces its own turning points in advance. The people living through the Bronze Age Collapse felt it, in the moment, as a string of ordinary decisions, urgent threats, and office responses. They did not call it "the Bronze Age Collapse," a label historians would only apply looking backward, centuries later, once the full pattern of loss across a whole region became clear. No scribe at Pylos ever wrote that word for what was happening to them, because from where they stood, nothing yet had a name.

📚 WHAT ENDURES, AND WHAT DOES NOT

As Quarter 1 closes, it is worth taking stock of what this crisis really swept away, and what it did not. The Hittite Empire ceases to exist as a political power. Hattusa itself is destroyed and never rebuilt as an imperial capital. Mycenaean palace civilization collapses so completely that writing itself vanishes from Greece for centuries. Its wanax-centered chain of officials and its Linear B writing disappear together. Other Mycenaean centers, including Tiryns and Mycenae itself, burn in this same rough span of years, though not always in exactly the same year. This is a reminder that even a single collapse comes in pieces, not all at once. Greece would not write in any form again for roughly four hundred years, until a new alphabet, borrowed from Phoenicia, took hold around 800 BC. This is a dark period this course will meet again in Week 16. Some estimates suggest Greece lost as much as three-quarters of its people over the century that followed.

Kassite Babylon falls as well. This opens up a Mesopotamian power vacuum that Weeks 10 through 17 will trace in detail, as new powers rise to fill it. Egypt and the rest of Mesopotamia survive, wounded, their reach and wealth smaller than before, but their civilizations continue. Cyprus, an island rich in copper just off the Levantine coast, comes through this period more intact than many of its neighbors. Its valuable copper trade was likely too useful for the wider crisis to fully cut off. The Levant, meanwhile, is resettled in these same troubled decades by new arrivals. These include the Philistines, likely tied to the Sea Peoples themselves. They also include the tribal groups that Week 13 will describe becoming the kingdom of Israel. And along the coast, one people is placed, almost by accident of survival and where it stood, to inherit much of the long-distance Mediterranean trade network Mycenaean sailors once ran. That people is Phoenicia, whose own great story begins in earnest in Week 15.

Nothing about any of this was planned, or owed to any civilization by the shape of history itself. Some things ended. Some things endured, changed by what they survived. Some new things were only now becoming possible. Quarter 2 opens on a Near East that would look, within a century, nothing like the world these first nine weeks have described.
