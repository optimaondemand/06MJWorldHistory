⚙️ TECHNOLOGY SPOTLIGHT · CUNEIFORM WRITING
Photo: LACMA, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

Long before anyone in Mesopotamia wrote a full sentence, temple officials used small clay tokens shaped like cones, spheres, and disks, each standing for an amount of grain, oil, or animals. By around 3300 BC, officials began pressing these tokens into wet clay, then drawing the token's shape directly into the clay with a reed stylus. Over centuries, these pictures grew simpler and could also stand for sounds, not just objects, becoming true writing. The wedge-shaped marks left by a reed stylus give this script its name: cuneiformkyoo-NEE-uh-forman early writing system made of wedge-shaped marks pressed into wet clay with a reed stylus., from the Latin word for "wedge-shaped." A person trained to read and write cuneiform, called a scribeSKRYBa person specially trained to read and write, one of the most respected jobs in Mesopotamian society., became one of the most valued and respected jobs in Mesopotamian society. Cuneiform was written in dozens of different tongues for roughly three thousand years, longer than any other writing system in world history has lasted.

📚 KINGSHIP DESCENDED FROM HEAVEN

Once cuneiform grew into a flexible tool, Sumerian scribes began writing down their own past. One of the most amazing records to survive is the Sumerian King List, a long record of cities and kings. In the scribes' own telling, this list stretches back before a great flood swept over the land. According to the list, "kingship descended from heaven" and was first held by the city of Eridu. From there, kingship passed from city to city among a handful of legendary rulers, reigning for tens of thousands of years each, before the flood came and swept everything away. After the flood, kingship "descended from heaven" a second time, passing to the city of Kish. From Kish onward, the reigns recorded shrink down to lengths a real human king could actually rule for.

What should a careful reader make of this? The King List mixes real historical memory, real cities in something close to a real order of political power, with clear legendLEJ-enda traditional story about the past that may contain some real memory, but is not treated as a strictly factual record.: reigns of impossible length, and a single giant flood that ends an entire age of the world. Sorting out these two threads with honesty, without tossing out either one, is one of the core skills of historical thinking.

Gilgamesh himself, the king credited with building Uruk's wall, is a perfect case of this mixing. He shows up on the King List as a real, historical ruler of Uruk's dynasty. Yet he is also the hero of the Epic of Gilgamesh, an epicEP-ika long poem about the adventures of a great hero, often mixing real memory with legend. poem built up over centuries. That poem gives him journeys, fights with monsters, and a quest for life without end that no historian treats as plain fact. A single figure can be both a real king remembered in an official record, and a legendary hero remembered in poetry, at the very same time.

This week's primary sources let you read both threads for yourself. Primary Source 1 is an excerpt from the Epic of Gilgamesh's own flood story, told by a survivor named Utnapishtim. Primary Source 2 is an excerpt from the Sumerian King List itself, including its own account of the flood and kingship's return afterward. As you read, ask yourself which parts feel like memory, and which feel like legend, and why that distinction matters.
