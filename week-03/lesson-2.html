📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why do empires fall apart, and who or what is really to blame?

Conquering dozens of independent Sumerian city-states, as Sargon did, is one problem. Governing them afterward, so they stay conquered, is a much harder one. This lesson looks at how the Akkadian Empire tried to solve that problem under Sargon's grandson, Naram-Sin, and then turns to a still-debated question: why, roughly a century and a half after Sargon's rise, did the empire collapse? An ancient poem blames one king's pride. Modern evidence points to drought and invasion. Both threads are worth taking seriously at once.

📚 STANDARDIZING AN EMPIRE

Sargon and his successors tried a truly new plan for ruling. The best-known of these successors was his grandson, Naram-Sin. Instead of leaving each conquered city fully to its own local rulers and customs, Sargon's dynasty put Akkadian-picked governors over conquered cities. They pushed the Akkadian language, a language apart from Sumerian, alongside Sumerian in state business, and worked to make weights, measures, and how things were run the same across the empire's many cities.

Naram-Sin went further than any Akkadian king before him. He took the title "King of the Four Quarters," meaning king of the whole known world. In some old tales, he even claimed to be a god himself during his own life. Kings before Naram-Sin mostly served the gods. Naram-Sin, in this telling, tried to stand next to them instead.

📚 THE CURSE OF AKKAD'S EXPLANATION

And then, within a few generations, the Akkadian Empire fell apart. A Sumerian poem called the Curse of Akkad, which you will read from directly this week, gives a sharp reason why. In the poem's telling, Naram-Sin defied the will of the great god Enlil and tore down Enlil's own temple, the Ekur at Nippur. In reply, Enlil cursed the city of Akkad itself, brought famine down on the land, and called on a fierce people from the mountains, the Gutians, to overrun it. In this telling, hubrisHYOO-brisa king or hero's dangerous, excessive pride, especially pride that leads someone to defy the gods or ignore real limits., a king's excess pride, calls down the wrath of the gods.

📚 WEIGHING AN ANCIENT EXPLANATION AGAINST A MODERN ONE

This old reason is worth taking with real weight, on its own terms, rather than pushing it aside. Modern historians work from clues the ancient poem's author never had. Studies of old soil and pollen layers show a long, hard drought hit the region around the time of Akkad's fall, which would have strained the canal-fed farming Sumer relied on. The poem itself also names Gutian raids from the Zagros Mountains, which historians think were most likely real events too, even though the poem frames them as payback sent by the gods.

Do not ask "which single cause is the whole answer?" A drought does not, by itself, force a king to defy the priests of Enlil. A moral flaw does not, by itself, dry up a river. Both threads were very likely at work together. Notice, too, that Akkad's fall does not fit a story of steady progress. Great empires can decline and fall apart; they do not simply grow stronger forever, and the reasons why are rarely simple. The Bronze Age Collapse in Week 9 will ask this same question again, at a much larger scale.

👥 PEOPLES · THE GUTIANS

Mesopotamian texts describe the Gutians as mountain people from the Zagros range, living outside the city-based world of Sumer and Akkad. Because nearly every surviving description of the Gutians was written by their enemies, historians treat these descriptions cautiously, as evidence of how Mesopotamians viewed outsiders as much as evidence of the Gutians themselves.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE · NARAM-SIN
Photo: Rama, CC BY-SA 2.0 France, via Wikimedia Commons.

Naram-Sin ruled the Akkadian Empire as Sargon's grandson, around 2254 to 2218 BC. He expanded the empire further than Sargon had, took the title "King of the Four Quarters," and, according to some ancient traditions, claimed divine status during his own lifetime. His famous victory stele shows him trampling defeated enemies while wearing the horned crown normally reserved for gods.

💪 KEEP THIS IN MIND

The first empire in recorded history rose on a string of small, uncertain events and fell for reasons that are still debated today. Hold both halves of that story together: Sargon's rise and Akkad's fall are the same lesson, that history does not march toward one guaranteed outcome, told twice.
