Ashurbanipal died around 627 BC. He was ruler of the largest empire the Near East had yet seen. Within fifteen years, his capital had burned to the ground. Within a generation, the Neo-Assyrian Empire itself had ceased to exist at all. This is one of the most sudden reversals of fortune this course will study. It deserves to be taken just as seriously as Assyria's rise was in Week 11. In other words, the empire's rise took generations. Its fall took only a few years.

📚 A FAST, UGLY SUCCESSION

Ashurbanipal's death was followed quickly by a succession crisisa dangerous period after a ruler dies, when rival heirs or officials fight over who takes power next.. His own sons and rival officials fought each other for the throne. This fighting weakened the central authority that had held Assyria's vast, many-peopled empire together for so long. This is exactly the kind of internal political failure Week 11 flagged as an open question. Could terror alone bind together the many different peoples, cities, and former kingdoms Assyria had conquered? Could it do that once the strong king who had inspired that terror was gone? The answer, playing out within just a few years of Ashurbanipal's death, was no. In other words, fear alone could not hold the empire together.

Vassal kingdoms and rival powers alike sensed weakness at once. Two of them moved fastest. Babylon, to the south, had grown restless under Assyrian rule for generations. This went back to the civil war Lesson 1 described, between Ashurbanipal and his own brother. The Medes were a people from the Zagros Mountains to the east, a group this course will meet in full in Week 14. They had their own long-standing complaint against Assyrian power. These two very different peoples formed an alliance against their common former master. It was a partnership neither side could have counted on, just a generation earlier. In other words, two old enemies of Assyria became allies against it. Babylon's own king, Nabopolassar, had already broken away from Assyrian rule in 626 BC, the year after Ashurbanipal's death. He spent the next several years fighting Assyrian armies alone, before the Medes agreed to join him. The two allies took the old religious city of Assur first, in 614 BC. They turned together on Nineveh itself two years later.

News of Nineveh's coming fall did not surprise everyone equally. A Hebrew prophet named Nahum wrote an entire short book celebrating the city's fall in advance. He called it long-deserved punishment for years of Assyrian cruelty. Judah, a kingdom this course will meet in full next week, had spent generations living in Assyria's shadow. Nahum's words capture just how many people across the region had reason to celebrate Assyria's fall, not mourn it. In other words, many people across the region were glad to see Assyria fall.

📚 THE FALL OF NINEVEH

This week's second Primary Source is drawn from a Babylonian chronicle. It records the campaign in the terse, factual style typical of Mesopotamian chronicles, listing marches, sieges, and outcomes with almost no editorializing at all. In 612 BC, the combined Babylonian-Median army besieged NinevehNIN-uh-vuhAshurbanipal's royal capital, on the Tigris River, home to his great library. itself, Ashurbanipal's great capital and the home of his library. The siege lasted roughly three months before the city fell. The Assyrian king at the time, Sin-shar-ishkun, is recorded as dying during the city's fall, though the chronicle's terse style leaves some of the exact details still uncertain.

Nineveh was thoroughly destroyed. It was never rebuilt as an imperial capital. This was a fate the Hittite Empire had already suffered, after the Bronze Age Collapse in Week 9. Here, though, it happened on a far larger scale, and after a far shorter warning. A surviving remnant of the Assyrian army did not simply vanish overnight. A new claimant, Ashur-uballit II, held out for a few more years at the western city of Harran. Egyptian troops backed him for a time. They wanted to keep some kind of Assyrian buffer standing between themselves and Babylon. Even that last foothold fell by 609 BC, closing the book on Assyrian rule for good. In other words, Assyria's fall did not happen all at once. It took a few more years to finish.

⚠️ A QUESTION WORTH ANSWERING DIRECTLY
This course has repeatedly asked whether a people's laws, customs, and habits explain their success or failure at first-order political problems. One of those problems stands out here above all: the ability to organize against outside threats. War is, in a real sense, the ultimate test of that ability. It is the one problem an empire cannot fake its way through with proud carvings and royal boasts alone. Assyria, at the height of its power under Ashurbanipal, looked as though it could not possibly fail that test. A single generation later, it failed completely. It collapsed faster than the slower Egyptian decline this course studied in Week 10. Terror had won Assyria an empire. But terror alone, it turns out, left nothing else behind. It did not build the shared political community that might have held that empire together. It did not build the bonds of loyalty and shared purpose an empire needs once its strongest king is gone. A library full of borrowed wisdom, gathered from every corner of Mesopotamia, could not save the throne room that housed it. In other words, all that knowledge could not save the throne that gathered it.
📚 WHAT COMES NEXT

Nineveh's fall did not end Mesopotamian empire itself. It only ended the Assyrian one. Babylon, one of the two winning powers in this campaign, was about to enter its own most famous and powerful period. It would happen under a king whose building projects would become legendary across the ancient world. Week 13 turns there directly.
