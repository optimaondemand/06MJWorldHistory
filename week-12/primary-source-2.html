Primary Source 2
The Babylonian Chronicle (on the Fall of Nineveh, 612 BC)
About this source: C.J. Gadd's 1923 publication and English translation of the "Fall of Nineveh" tablet, British Museum No. 21901 (The Fall of Nineveh: The Newly Discovered Babylonian Chronicle No. 21901 in the British Museum, Oxford University Press for the British Museum, 1923). Public domain. A terse, year-by-year record typical of Mesopotamian chronicle writing, listing events with little commentary or explanation.

Gadd's own summary of the chronicle's entries for these events:
"Junction effected between Babylonians, Medes, and Scythians. March against Nineveh." ... "Siege of Nineveh pressed; three battles." ... "Capture of Nineveh. Death of Sin-shar-ishkun and many commanders." The city, Gadd notes, was "turned into a mound and a ruin." The chronicle then records the "Departure of Kyaxares" (the Median king) on the twentieth day of the month of Elul, with Nabopolassar of Babylon receiving further booty before the armies withdrew.

On the siege's length, per the chronicle's own dating:
The armies "encamped against Nineveh. From the month Sivan until the month Ab, for three months, they subjected the city to a heavy siege," before "Sin-shar-ishkun, king of Assyria," met his end and the city's booty was carried off.

📝 EDITORIAL NOTE ON THIS CHRONICLE'S STYLE
Notice how little explanation or emotion this text offers, even while describing the fall of one of the ancient world's largest empires. Babylonian chronicles were kept as factual, year-by-year administrative records, not as dramatic narratives, which is precisely why historians treat them as valuable but also why they leave so many specific details, including the exact fate of Nineveh's last king, genuinely uncertain.
